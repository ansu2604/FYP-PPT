Overview
Abstract

The rapid digitization of judicial records has created a huge increase in the availability of legal petitions, judgments, and case documents. Manual interpretation and classification of such documents is slow, expensive, and requires high expertise — making automated legal document processing an important research area.

This project focuses on developing an intelligent system for legal petition classification using transformer-based deep learning models such as BERT, DistilBERT, and LegalBERT. The system predicts whether a legal petition is likely to be Allowed or Dismissed.

Since legal datasets often contain limited labeled samples, this work incorporates a semi-supervised learning approach using pseudo-labeling to improve performance by utilizing additional unlabeled data. The system also integrates Explainable AI (XAI) methods that extract top evidence sentences, enabling human-understandable reasoning for each prediction.

The system is trained and evaluated using the ILDC (Indian Legal Documents Corpus) dataset, and a lightweight interactive interface is designed using Streamlit to display predictions, confidence scores, and explanation highlights.

Motivation
The Core Problem
Why automated legal petition classification matters — and what makes it hard.
⚖️

Judicial Backlog

India's judicial system has millions of pending cases — from civil disputes to constitutional cases — causing painful delays in justice. Manual classification by legal experts is simply unsustainable at this scale.

📄

Complex Legal Language

Legal documents contain highly specialized vocabulary, intricate sentence structures, and context-dependent references. Traditional NLP models like TF-IDF + SVM lack the contextual understanding required for accurate analysis.

🔒

Limited Labeled Data

Annotating legal documents requires domain experts — making labeled data expensive and scarce. Models that rely purely on supervised learning struggle with this data bottleneck.

🕵️

Black-Box Decisions

When an AI's decision affects someone's legal future, judges and lawyers must understand why that decision was made. Current systems lack the transparency required for trustworthy deployment in legal settings.

Data Preprocessing

The ILDC dataset is loaded via Pandas and Hugging Face. Steps include removing null values, cleaning special symbols, tokenization using the BERT tokenizer, and truncating long documents to fit model input limits.

🤖

Transformer Fine-Tuning

Pre-trained models (BERT, DistilBERT, LegalBERT) are fine-tuned with an added classification layer. The AdamW optimizer is used. The dataset is split into training and validation sets for performance tracking.

🔄

Semi-Supervised Learning (Pseudo-Labeling)

  1. Train model on labeled documents
  2. Predict labels for unlabeled documents
  3. Accept predictions with ≥ 90% confidence
  4. Re-train model with pseudo-labeled data
  5. Repeat until convergence
💡

Explainability Module (XAI)

The legal document is split into sentences. Transformer embeddings capture the meaning of each sentence. The most influential sentences are highlighted as evidence — giving human-readable reasoning for each prediction.

Key Findings

  • LegalBERT outperforms DistilBERT across all evaluation metrics due to domain-specific pretraining on legal corpora.
  • Both models perform better at identifying dismissed petitions — reflecting the class imbalance (4,399 dismissed vs 3,194 allowed).
  • Pseudo-labeling improved model robustness by incorporating high-confidence unlabeled predictions into the training set.
  • The explainability module increases credibility by highlighting evidence sentences that justify each prediction.
  • Truncation of long legal documents (due to transformer token limits) remains a key challenge — a direction for future work.
